## PART I: URDU GRAMMAR (Urdu Qawaid)

> For every grammar point, examples include Urdu script, Roman transliteration, and English translation.

## 1. Urdu Language Overview

Urdu is an Indo-Aryan language with strong Persian, Arabic, and Turkic influences. It shares its grammar base with Hindi (both descend from Hindustani/Khari Boli) but uses the Nastaliq script (right-to-left) and draws its formal vocabulary from Persian and Arabic.

**Key distinguishing features:**
- **SOV word order** (Subject-Object-Verb) — unlike English's SVO
- **Postpositions** instead of prepositions
- **Grammatical gender** — masculine and feminine
- **Ergative-absolutive alignment** in past perfective tenses (the نے /ne/ marker)
- **Three-case system** — nominative (direct), oblique, vocative
- **Aspect-based tense system** — habitual, progressive, perfective

The script: 39 letters, written right-to-left in the Nastaliq calligraphic style.

---

## 2. Sentence Structure (SOV)

Urdu follows **Subject + Object + Verb** order for all basic sentences.

| English (SVO) | Urdu (SOV) |
|---------------|-----------|
| I eat an apple. | مَیں سَب کھاتا ہوں۔ (main seb khātā hū̃) |
| She reads a book. | وہ کِتاب پَڑھتی ہے۔ (voh kitāb paṛhtī hai) |
| The boy plays football. | لَڑکا فُٹ بال کھیلتا ہے۔ (laṛkā football kheltā hai) |
| The cat drinks milk. | بِلّی دودھ پیتی ہے۔ (billī dūdh pītī hai) |

**Rule:** The verb ALWAYS goes at the end of the sentence. Modifiers (adjectives, adverbs) come before the words they modify:
- Adjective-Noun: `achā laṛkā` (good boy), `khūbsūrat phūl` (beautiful flower)
- Adverb-Verb: `āhista bolnā` (speak slowly), `bohat achhā` (very good)
- Time adverbs at sentence start: `kal main āyā thā` (Yesterday I came)

## 3. Grammatical Gender (Jins)

Every Urdu noun has one of two genders. This affects verb conjugation, adjective agreement, and pronoun usage.

| Gender | Urdu Term | Endings | Examples |
|--------|-----------|---------|----------|
| **Masculine** (مذکر) | Muzakkar | Often ends in: `ـا aa`, consonant, `ـہ` (sometimes) | لڑکا laṛkā (boy), گھر ghar (house), درخت darakht (tree) |
| **Feminine** (مؤنث) | Mu'annas | Often ends in: `ـی ii`, `ـاں aan`, `ـت/ـش` | لڑکی laṛkī (girl), کتاب kitāb (book), ماں mā̃ (mother) |

**Gender change rules:**
- Masculine `ā` → Feminine `ī`: `laṛkā → laṛkī`, `beṭā → beṭī` (son/daughter)
- Many inanimate objects have arbitrary gender: `دل dil` (heart) = M, `بات bāt` (matter) = F

**Impact on verbs:**
```
لڑکا کھیلتا ہے۔  (laṛkā kheltā hai)    — The boy plays (Masc)
لڑکی کھیلتی ہے۔  (laṛkī kheltī hai)    — The girl plays (Fem)
مَیں آیا۔  (main āyā)          — I came (male speaker)
مَیں آئی۔  (main ā'ī)          — I came (female speaker)
```

## 4. Noun Declension (Number & Case)

Urdu has three cases: **nominative** (direct — dictionary form), **oblique** (used before any postposition), and **vocative** (used for direct address).

### Masculine Type-I (ends in -ā)

| Case | Singular | Urdu | Plural | Urdu |
|------|----------|------|--------|------|
| **Nominative** | laṛkā | لڑکا | laṛke | لڑکے |
| **Oblique** | laṛke | لڑکے | laṛkõ | لڑکوں |
| **Vocative** | laṛke | لڑکے | laṛko | لڑکو |

### Feminine Type-I (ends in -ī)

| Case | Singular | Urdu | Plural | Urdu |
|------|----------|------|--------|------|
| **Nominative** | laṛkī | لڑکی | laṛkiyā̃ | لڑکیاں |
| **Oblique** | laṛkī | لڑکی | laṛkiyõ | لڑکیوں |
| **Vocative** | laṛkī | لڑکی | laṛkiyo | لڑکیو |

### Masculine Type-II (ends in consonant)

| Case | Singular | Urdu | Plural | Urdu |
|------|----------|------|--------|------|
| **Nominative** | ghar | گھر | ghar | گھر |
| **Oblique** | ghar | گھر | gharõ | گھروں |
| **Vocative** | ghar | گھر | gharo | گھرو |

### Feminine Type-II (ends in consonant)

| Case | Singular | Urdu | Plural | Urdu |
|------|----------|------|--------|------|
| **Nominative** | kitāb | کتاب | kitābẽ | کتابیں |
| **Oblique** | kitāb | کتاب | kitābõ | کتابوں |
| **Vocative** | kitāb | کتاب | kitābo | کتابو |

**Key Rule:** The noun takes the **oblique form** before any postposition.
- Nominative: `laṛkā` → لڑکا
- Oblique before postposition: `laṛke ne` → لڑکے نے, `laṛke kā` → لڑکے کا

**Vocative usage in poetry:**
- لڑکے! سن۔ *laṛke! sun.* — Boy! Listen.
- اے دوست! *ae dost!* — O friend!
- اے محبوب! *ae mahbūb!* — O beloved!

## 5. Postpositions (Case Markers)

Urdu uses **postpositions** (words that come AFTER the noun) to indicate grammatical case.

| Case | Postposition | Function | Urdu Example | English |
|------|-------------|----------|-------------|---------|
| **Nominative** | ∅ (no marker) | Subject (unmarked) | لڑکا laṛkā | the boy |
| **Oblique** | ∅ (before postpos) | Used when any postposition follows | لڑکے laṛke | (oblique form) |
| **Ergative** | نے ne | Subject of transitive past tense | لڑکے نے laṛke ne | (by) the boy |
| **Accusative/Dative** | کو ko | Object (specific/direct), or "to/for" | لڑکے کو laṛke ko | to the boy |
| **Instrumental** | سے se | "with, by, from" | لڑکے سے laṛke se | from/by the boy |
| **Genitive** | کا kā/کی kī/کے ke | Possession (agrees with possessed noun) | لڑکے کا laṛke kā | boy's |
| **Inessive** | میں mẽ | "in, inside" | گھر میں ghar mẽ | in the house |
| **Adessive** | پر par | "on, upon, at" | میز پر maiz par | on the table |

### Genitive Agreement

The genitive postposition **agrees with the POSSESSED noun** (the thing owned):

| Possessed | Form | Example | English |
|-----------|------|---------|---------|
| Masc. sg | کا kā | لڑکے کا گھر | the boy's house |
| Masc. pl / oblique | کے ke | لڑکے کے گھر | the boy's houses |
| Feminine | کی kī | لڑکے کی کتاب | the boy's book |

### Compound Postpositions

| Postposition | Meaning | Example |
|-------------|---------|---------|
| کے لیے *ke liye* | for | تمہارے لیے |
| کے ساتھ *ke sāth* | with | میرے ساتھ |
| کے بغیر *ke baghair* | without | پانی کے بغیر |
| کے بارے میں *ke bāre mẽ* | about | اس کے بارے میں |
| کے نیچے *ke nīche* | under | میز کے نیچے |
| کے اوپر *ke ūpar* | above | گھر کے اوپر |
| کی طرح *kī tarah* | like | شیر کی طرح |
| تک *tak* | until, up to | گھر تک |

## 7. Adjectives and Agreement

Adjectives **precede** nouns. **Marked adjectives** (ending in -ā) agree in gender/number; **unmarked adjectives** (ending in consonant) do not.

| Adjective | Masc Singular | Masc Plural | Feminine |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|----------|
| اچھا achhā (good) | اچھا لڑکا | اچھے لڑکے | اچھی لڑکی |
| بڑا baṛā (big) | بڑا گھر | بڑے گھر | بڑی مسجد |
| لمبا lambā (tall) | لمبا درخت | لمبے درخت | لمبی عورت |
| نیا nayā (new) | نیا مکان | نئے مکان | نئی گاڑی |

**Unmarked adjectives** (no change): خوبصورت *khūbsūrat* (beautiful), بہادر *bahādur* (brave), صاف *sāf* (clean), ایماندار *īmāndār* (honest)

**Oblique form:** Before a postposition, marked adjectives take the oblique ending:
- اچھے لڑکے *سے* — achhe laṛke *se* (from the good boy)
- بڑے گھر *میں* — baṛe ghar *mẽ* (in the big house)

**Comparison:**
- Comparative: X **سے** + adjective — *is se achhā* (better than this)
- Superlative: **سب سے** + adjective — *sab se achhā* (best of all)

### 8.4 Present Progressive

Formation: stem + **رہا/رہی/رہے** + present auxiliary

| Urdu | Transliteration | English |
|------|----------------|---------|
| وہ کتاب پڑھ رہی ہے۔ | voh kitāb paṛh rahī hai. | She is reading a book. |
| مَیں خط لکھ رہا ہوں۔ | main khat likh rahā hū̃. | I am writing a letter. |

### 8.5 Past Tense — Two Systems

#### A. Past Habitual ("used to")

Stem + **تا/تی/تے** + past auxiliary:
- مَیں پڑھتا تھا *main paṛhtā thā* (I used to study)
- وہ آتی تھی *voh ātī thī* (She used to come)

#### B. Past Progressive ("was doing")

Stem + **رہا/رہی/رہے** + past auxiliary:
- وہ پڑھ رہا تھا *voh paṛh rahā thā* (He was reading)

#### C. Past Perfective — THE ERGATIVE CONSTRUCTION

This is the most important rule in Urdu grammar:

**Intransitive verbs** (go, come, sleep): verb agrees with **subject**.
- لڑکا آیا۔ *laṛkā āyā.* (The boy came)
- لڑکی آئی۔ *laṛkī ā'ī.* (The girl came)

**Transitive verbs** (read, write, eat, do): subject takes **نے** *ne* (ergative), verb agrees with **OBJECT**.
- لڑکے نے کتاب پڑھی۔ *laṛke ne kitāb paṛhī.* — verb agrees with *kitāb* (fem) → *paṛhī*
- اس نے خط لکھا۔ *us ne khat likhā.* — verb agrees with *khat* (masc) → *likhā*
- مَیں نے کتابیں پڑھیں۔ *main ne kitābẽ paṛhī̃.* — plural feminine → *paṛhī̃*

**If the object is ko-marked** (definite animate), verb defaults to masculine singular:
- لڑکے نے لڑکی **کو** دیکھا۔ *laṛke ne laṛkī ko dekhā.* (verb = masc.sg)

### 8.6 Present Perfect

Perfective participle + present auxiliary:
- اس نے خط لکھا ہے۔ *us ne khat likhā hai.* (He has written a letter)

## 9. Negation

**نہیں** *nahī̃* before the main verb (not before the auxiliary):
- مَیں نہیں جاتا۔ *main nahī̃ jātā.* (I don't go)
- وہ نہیں آیا۔ *voh nahī̃ āyā.* (He didn't come)

**نہ** *na* is used for prohibitions and subjunctive negation (common in poetry):
- یہ نہ کرو۔ *yeh na karo.* (Don't do this)
- کاش وہ نہ آئے۔ *kāsh voh na ā'e.* (If only he wouldn't come)

In negative past transitive, *ne* is optionally dropped in colloquial speech.

## 10. Core Structural Elements of Urdu Poetry

### 10.1 Misra (مصرع) — A Line
A single line of poetry. Each sher (couplet) has two misras:
- **Misra-e-Oola** (مصرع اولی) — The first line
- **Misra-e-Saani** (مصرع ثانی) — The second line

### 10.2 Sher (شعر) — A Couplet
A complete two-line unit. A good sher is **self-contained** — it expresses a complete thought independent of the other shers in the ghazal. It must:
- Follow the same **Bahr** (meter) as every other sher in the ghazal
- End with the same **Radeef** and **Qafiya** pattern
- Be grammatically and musically complete within itself

### 10.3 Ghazal (غزل) — Collection of Shers
- Minimum **5 shers** (up to ~15 traditionally, ~7 most common)
- All shers share: **same Bahr**, **same Radeef**, **same Qafiya**
- Shers are **thematically independent** — no narrative thread required
- Has no title; indexed/remembered by its Radeef

### 10.4 Matla (مطلع) — Opening Sher
The first sher of a ghazal. **Both misras** contain the Qafiya + Radeef.
- If the Radeef appears in both lines → it's the Matla
- A ghazal can have multiple Matla-like shers (Matla-e-Saani / Husn-e-Matla)

### 10.5 Maqta (مقطع) — Closing Sher
The last sher containing the poet's **Takhallus** (تخلص — pen name).
- The poet inserts their name, often as wordplay
- Example — Ghalib ends with "Ghalib", Faraz ends with "Faraz"
- Not strictly mandatory but a strong tradition

### 10.6 Takhallus (تخلص) — Pen Name
Every Urdu poet adopts a pen name that appears in the Maqta.
- Ghalib → غالب (The Dominant / Victorious)
- Mir → میر
- Faraz → فراز (The Elevated)
- Faiz → فیض (Grace / Bounty)
- Iqbal → اقبال (Fortune)
- Daag → داغ (Blemish/Stain)
- Jigar → جگر (Liver / Heart)

## 11. Rhyme System: Qafiya & Radeef

### 11.1 Radeef (ردیف) — The Refrain
A word or phrase **repeated unchanged** at the end of every sher's second misra (and both misras of the Matla).

**Example** (Ghalib's famous ghazal):
```
koii ummiid bar nahiiN aatii
koii suurat nazar nahiiN aatii
...
jaanataa huN savaab-e-taa'at-o-zahad
par tabiiyat idhar nahiiN aatii
```
Here **"nahiiN aatii"** is the Radeef — it ends every second line identically.

### 11.2 Qafiya (قافیہ) — The Rhyme
The **rhyming word(s) that immediately precede the Radeef**. Each sher must use a different Qafiya word, but all Qafiyas must share the same **rhyme sound**.

From the Ghalib example: `ummiid` → `suurat` → `idhar` → `kisii` → `savaab` → `tabiiyat`

**Cleaner Qafiya example:**
```
be-qararii, taarii, guzarii, bhaarii, tumhaarii
```
All end in `-arii` — tight, perfect rhyme.

### 11.3 Qafiya Rules
1. Must have the **same metrical weight (wazn)** in every sher
2. Must rhyme in terms of the **last consonant + preceding vowel sound**
3. Can be one word or a compound
4. In the Matla, both misras have Qafiya + Radeef; in subsequent shers, only the second misra
5. The Qafiya must be a different word each time — never repeat the same qafiya word

## 12. Meter & Prosody (Ilm-e-Arooz / علم عروض)

Ilm-e-Arooz is the science of poetic meter in Urdu. It was formalized by Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi in 8th-century Arabia and adapted for Persian and Urdu.

### 12.1 Bahr (بحر) — The Meter
The rhythmic pattern of a ghazal. All shers in a ghazal must be in the **same Bahr**. Bahr literally means "sea" — it's the rhythmic ocean in which the poem flows.

### 12.2 Wazn (وزن) — Syllable Weight & The Sabab-Watad System

Ilm-e-Arooz analyzes syllable weight through two fundamental building blocks that combine to form the arkan:

#### A. Sabab (سبب) — A Two-Syllable Element
A sabab consists of two consonants (with or without vowels) and comes in two types:

| Type | Name | Pattern | Weight | Example |
|------|------|---------|--------|---------|
| Light Sabab | **Sabab-e-Khafeef** (سبب خفیف) | مُتَحَرِّك + سَاكِن (1+1→"2") | 2 | `tu` in `tum`, `ka` in `kal` |
| Heavy Sabab | **Sabab-e-Saqeel** (سبب ثقیل) | مُتَحَرِّك + مُتَحَرِّك (1+1) | 1+1 | `ka` + `ra` in `kara` |

In practice, Sabab-e-Khafeef (consonant + short vowel → consonant) counts as **weight 2**, while Sabab-e-Saqeel (consonant + short vowel + consonant + short vowel) counts as **two weight-1 syllables**.

#### B. Watad (وتد) — A Three-Syllable Element
A watad consists of three consonants/vowels:

| Type | Name | Pattern | Weight | Example |
|------|------|---------|--------|---------|
| **Watad-e-Majmua** (وتد مجموع) | 1+2 | حَرَكَة + حَرَكَة + سَاكِن | 1-2 | `fa-uu` in `fa-uu-lun` |
| **Watad-e-Mafruq** (وتد مفروق) | 2+1 | حَرَكَة + سَاكِن + حَرَكَة | 2-1 | `faa-i` in `faa-i-lun` |

#### C. Syllable Weight Grid (Detailed)

Every Urdu syllable is classified by weight (wazn) for scansion purposes:

| Pattern | Type | Weight | Examples |
|---------|------|--------|----------|
| Single consonant | متحرک (mutaharrik) | 1 | `j` in `aaj`, `m` in `main` |
| Consonant + short vowel (CV) | متحرک | 1 | `ka`, `tu`, `se`, `mi`, `ki` |
| Consonant + short vowel + consonant (CVC) | ساکن (saakin) | 2 | `dil`, `tum`, `chal`, `kar`, `gham` |
| Long vowel alone (Vː) | ساکن | 2 | `aa`, `ii`, `uu`, `o`, `e`, `ai` |
| Consonant + long vowel (CVː) | ساکن | 2 | `jaa`, `ho`, `de`, `raa`, `see` |
| Long vowel + consonant (CVːC) | Overlong | 2+1* | `yaad` = 2+1, `baat` = 2+1, `sharm` = 2+1 |
| Consonant + long vowel + consonant (CVːC) | Overlong | 2+1* | `kaam`, `jaan`, `raaz`, `naam`, `ghaam` |
| Two consonants together (cluster) | ساكنان | 2 | `ab`, `in`, `un` (vowel already short) |
| Short vowel followed by double consonant | ساكنان | 1+2 | `tum` (tu=1, um=2 in scansion) |

*\*Overlong syllables are subdivided as 2+1 in scansion — the extra half-beat is crucial for matching arkan.*

#### D. Key Weight Rules & Conventions

1. **The Short-to-Long Rule**: A short syllable (weight 1) followed by a consonant becomes weight 2 (the consonant closes the syllable).

2. **The Overlong Rule**: A long vowel + consonant = 2+1 in taqti. This means overlong syllables can span across rukn boundaries. Example: `yaad` in `yaad hai` breaks as `yaa`=2 (end of one rukn) + `d`=1 (start of next).

3. **Final Syllable Flexibility**: In Urdu prosody (unlike Persian), any final long vowel can be **shortened** to fit the meter. Example: In Ghalib's `kaabaa`, the final `aa` is shortened when needed.

4. **The Two-Consonant Rule**: When two consonants appear together (e.g., `b` in `kabhi` → `ka-bhii`), the first consonant closes the preceding syllable (making it weight 2), and the second opens the next syllable.

5. **Elision & Hastening** (Takhfeef): A syllable that is normally weight 2 can be treated as weight 1 if the poet "hastens" its pronunciation. This is called *takhfeef* (تخفیف) and is a recognized poetic license — but overuse marks poor craftsmanship.

6. **Silent/Quiescent Letters** (Harf-e-Saakin): A consonant with no vowel (sukun) always counts as closing the previous syllable, adding weight.

### 12.3 Arkan (ارکان) / Tafaeel (تفعیل) — Metrical Feet

The building blocks of Bahr. Eight standard arkan in Urdu poetry:

| Rukn | Syllable Pattern | Weight Pattern |
|------|-----------------|---------------|
| fa-uu-lun (فعولن) | short-long-long | 1-2-2 |
| faa-i-lun (فاعلن) | long-short-long | 2-1-2 |
| ma-faa-ii-lun (مفاعیلن) | short-long-long-long | 1-2-2-2 |
| mus-taf-i-lun (مستفعلن) | long-long-short-long | 2-2-1-2 |
| faa-i-laa-tun (فاعلاتن) | long-short-long-long | 2-1-2-2 |
| mu-ta-faa-i-lun (متفاعلن) | short-short-long-short-long | 1-1-2-1-2 |
| ma-faa-i-la-tun (مفاعلتن) | short-long-short-short-long | 1-2-1-1-2 |
| maf-uu-laat (مفعولات) | long-long-long | 2-2-2 (or 2-2-3) |

### 12.4 Zihaaf (زحاف) — Metrical Modifications

A rukn can be modified through *Zihaaf* (plural: *Zihaafaat* / *Zahifaat*), literally "alteration" — changes that shorten, lengthen, or shift syllables within a rukn. A modified rukn is called *Muzaahaf* (مزاحف), and a bahr built from modified arkan is a *Muzaahif* (مزاحف) bahr.

There are **three categories** of zihaaf:

- **Khaas Zihaaf** (خاص زحاف) — applies to only ONE specific rukn
- **Aam Zihaaf** (عام زحاف) — applies to MORE THAN ONE rukn
- **Murakkab Zihaaf** (مرکب زحاف) — combination of two or more zihaaf applied to one rukn

From 8 basic arkan, the application of zihaaf produces over **40 variants**, of which ~20 are commonly used in Urdu poetry.

#### A. Aam Zihaaf (Common Zihaaf — Apply to Multiple Arkan)

| # | Zihaaf Name | Meaning | Effect | Example |
|:-:|-------------|---------|--------|---------|
| 1 | **Hazf** (حذف) | Deletion | Drop the last **Sabab-e-Khafeef** (last 2-weight syllable) from a rukn. The modified bahr is called **Mahzuuf** (محذوف). | `fa-uu-lun` (1-2-2) → drop `lun` → `fa-uu` → replaced by `fa'` (2) |
| 2 | **Hazz** (حَذّ) | Cutting off | Drop the last **Watad-e-Majmua** (1-2) from a rukn. Modified bahr: **Ahazz** (أحذ). | `fa-uu-lun` → drop `uu-lun` → only `fa` remains → replaced by `fa'` (2) |
| 3 | **Khabn** (خبن) | Shortening | Drop the **second letter** (the saakin) of a Sabab-e-Khafeef at the **beginning** of a rukn. Modified: **Makhbuun** (مخبون). | `fa-i-lun` (2-1-2) → drop `i` → `fa-lun` → `fa`(1)+`lun`(2) = `fa` is now 1, giving `1-2` |
| 4 | **Qabz** (قبض) | Seizing | Drop the **5th letter** (a saakin letter) from a rukn. Modified: **Maqbuuz** (مقبوض). | `fa-uu-lun` (1-2-2) → drop `n` → `fa-uu-lu` (1-2-1) |
| 5 | **Kaff** (کف) | Withholding | Drop the **7th saakin letter** (usually the final `n`) from a rukn. Modified: **Makfuuf** (مکفوف). | `ma-faa-ii-lun` (1-2-2-2) → drop final `n` → `ma-faa-ii-lu` (1-2-2-1) |
| 6 | **Qasr** (قصر) | Shortening | From end of rukn: drop the **last saakin letter** and make the **second-last letter saakin**. Modified: **Maqsuur** (مقصور). | `ma-faa-ii-lun` (1-2-2-2) → drop `n`, make `l` saakin → `ma-faa-ii-l` (1-2-2-2 but final 2 is closed CVːC) |
| 7 | **Qata'** (قطع) | Cutting | If a rukn ends with Watad-e-Majmua (1-2), drop its last letter and make the second-last saakin. Modified: **Maqtua** (مقطوع). | `faa-i-lun` (2-1-2) → drop `n`, make `l` saakin → `faa-i-l` → replaced by `fe-lun` (2-2) |
| 8 | **Tayy** (طی) | Folding | If a rukn has two Sabab-e-Khafeef at its end, drop the **4th letter**. Modified: **Matvi** (مطوی). | `mus-taf-i-lun` (2-2-1-2) → drop `f` → `mus-ta-i-lun` (2-1-1-2) |
| 9 | **Izaalah** (ازالہ) | Removal/Addition | If a rukn ends with a watad, add an **alif** (ا) to the second-last letter. Modified: **Mazaal** (مزال). | `mus-taf-i-lun` → +`a` → `mus-taf-i-laan` (2-2-1-2) |
| 10 | **Tasbeegh** (تسبیغ) | Lengthening | If a rukn ends with Sabab-e-Khafeef, add **alif** to second-last. Modified: **Musabbagh** (مسبّغ) or **Muzaaf** (مزاف). | `faa-i-laa-tun` (2-1-2-2) → `faa-i-laa-taan` → replaced by `faa-i-li-yaan` (2-1-1-2) |
| 11 | **Waqf** (وقف) | Stoppage | If rukn ends in Watad-e-Mafruq (2-1), make the **last letter saakin**. Modified: **Mauquuf** (موقوف). | `maf-uu-laa-tu` (2-2-2-1) → make `tu` saakin → `maf-uu-laat` (2-2-3) |

#### B. Khaas Zihaaf (Specific Zihaaf — Apply to Single Arkan Only)

| # | Zihaaf Name | Rukn Applied To | Effect | Modified Bahr Name |
|:-:|-------------|-----------------|--------|-------------------|
| 12 | **Salm** (سلم) | `fa-uu-lun` (1-2-2) | Drop `fa` → `uu-lun` (2-2) remains, replaced by `fe-lun` (2-2) | **Aslam** (اسلم) |
| 13 | **Jabb** (جبّ) | `ma-faa-ii-lun` (1-2-2-2) | Drop `ii-lun` → `ma-fa` (1-2) remains, replaced by `fa-'` (2) | **Majbuub** (مجبوب) |
| 14 | **Kharm** (خرم) | `ma-faa-ii-lun` (1-2-2-2) | Drop `ma` → `faa-ii-lun` (2-2-2) remains, replaced by `ma-fuu-lun` (2-2-2) | **Akhram** (اخرم) |
| 15 | **Kashf** (کشف) | `maf-uu-laat` (2-2-3) | Drop final `t` → `maf-uu-laa` (2-2-2) remains, replaced by `maf-uu-lon` (2-2-2) | **Makshuuf** (مکشوف) |

#### C. Murakkab Zihaaf (Compound Zihaaf — Multiple Modifiers)

| # | Name | Combination | Applied To | Result | Bahr Name |
|:-:|------|-------------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| 16 | **Kharb** (خرب) | Kharm + Kaff | `ma-faa-ii-lun` → drop `ma` (Kharm) + drop `n` (Kaff) → `faa-ii-lu` | `faa-ii-lu` (2-2-1) | **Akhrab** (اخرب) |
| 17 | **Shatr** (شطر) | Kharm + Qabz | `ma-faa-ii-lun` → drop `ma` (Kharm) + drop `i` (Qabz) → `faa-i-lun` | `faa-i-lun` (2-1-2) | **Ashtar** (اشتر) |
| 18 | **Shakl** (شکل) | Khabn + Kaff | `faa-i-laa-tun` (2-1-2-2) → drop 2nd letter (Khabn) + final `n` (Kaff) → `f-i-laa-tu` | `f-i-laa-tu` (1-2-2-1) | **Mashkuul** (مشکول) |
| 19 | **Kasf** (کسف) | Waqf + Kaff | `maf-uu-laa-tu` → Waqf makes `maf-uu-laat`, Kaff drops final `t` → `maf-uu-laa` | `maf-uu-lon` (2-2-2) | **Maksuuf** (مکسوف) |
| 20 | **Hatm** (حطم) | Hazf + Qasr | `ma-faa-ii-lun` → drop `lun` (Hazf) → `ma-faa-ii` + apply Qasr → `ma-faa` | `faa-ul` (2-2) | **Ahtam** (احطم) |
| 21 | **'Aql** (عقل) | Tayy + Kaff | `mus-taf-i-lun` → Tayy drops `f` → `mus-ta-i-lun` + Kaff drops `n` | `mus-ta-i-lu` (2-1-1-1) | **Ma'quul** (معقول) |

#### D. How Zihaaf Names Appear in Bahr Names

When a bahr name contains multiple modifiers, they appear in the order the zihaaf were applied:

| Bahr Name Components | Meaning |
|---------------------|---------|
| Musaddas (مسدس) | 3 feet per misra (6-footed in total for a sher) |
| Musamman (مثمن) | 4 feet per misra (8-footed in a sher) |
| Murabba' (مربّع) | 2 feet per misra |
| Saalim (سالم) | All arkan pure (no zihaaf) |
| Mahzuuf (محذوف) | Modified by Hazf (last foot shortened) |
| Makhbuun (مخبون) | Modified by Khabn |
| Maqbuuz (مقبوض) | Modified by Qabz |
| Makfuuf (مکفوف) | Modified by Kaff |
| Maqsuur (مقصور) | Modified by Qasr |
| Maqtua (مقطوع) | Modified by Qata' |
| Akhrab (اخرب) | Modified by Kharm + Kaff |
| Ashtar (اشتر) | Modified by Kharm + Qabz |
| Akhram (اخرم) | Modified by Kharm alone |

**Example breakdown**: `Bahr-e-Ramal Musamman Mahzuuf` = Ramal meter, 4 feet per line, last foot shortened by Hazf.

### 12.5 Most Commonly Used Bahrs — Complete Reference (14 Bahrs)

Below are the 14 most commonly used bahrs in Urdu poetry. Each entry includes: technical name, arkan pattern, weight pattern, transliteration pattern, and a classic Urdu example with scansion.

#### Bahr #1 — Bahr-e-Hazaj Musamman Saalim (بحر ہزج مثمن سالم)
The most majestic bahr, used for grand philosophical and spiritual themes.

| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| **Rukn** | مفاعیلن (ma-faa-ii-lun) × 4 per misra |
| **Weight Pattern** | `1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2` |
| **Total Weight** | 28 per misra |
| **Feel** | Sweeping, epic, contemplative |

**Classic — Allama Iqbal:**
```
مٹا دے اپنی ہستى کو اگر کچھ مرتبہ چاہے
کہ دانا خاک میں مل کر گلِ گلزار ہوتا ہے
```
Scansion: `mi-Ṭaa-de-ap-nii-has-tii-ko-a-gar-kuchh-mar-ta-baa-chaa-he`
Weight: `1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2` ✓
Transliteration: *miṭā de apnī hastī ko agar kuchh martabā chāhe / ki dānā ḳhāk meñ mil kar gul-e-gulzār hotā hai*

**Another — Mast Kalkatavi:**
```
بھری دنیا سہی لیکن ٹھکانا ہم بھی پا لیں گے
جہاں دو گز زمین ہوگی وہیں ہم گھر بنا لیں گے
```
Weight: `1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2-2` ✓

### 12.6 Taqti (تقطیع) — Scansion: Step-by-Step Methodology

Taqti (literally "cutting into pieces") is the systematic process of breaking a misra into its constituent syllables and assigning weights to determine the bahr. It is the most essential technical skill in Urdu prosody.

#### The 5-Step Taqti Method

**Step 1 — Syllabify (ترنم)**
Speak the line naturally (tarannum), then write it syllable-by-syllable with hyphens. Key rules:
- Each consonant takes a vowel: `cha-lay` not `chaly`
- Double consonants split: `kab-hii` → `ka-bhii` (first closes previous syllable)
- Long vowels are their own syllable: `aa`, `ii`, `uu`

**Step 2 — Assign weights (وزن دو)**

| Pattern | Weight | Examples |
|---------|:------:|----------|
| CV (short vowel) | 1 | `ka`, `tu`, `se`, `ki`, `na`, `ga` |
| CVC (short vowel + cons) | 2 | `dil`, `tum`, `gham`, `husn` |
| CVː (long open vowel) | 2 | `jaa`, `hii`, `ho`, `daa`, `aa` |
| CVːC (overlong) | 2+1 | `yaad`=`yaa`(2)+`d`(1), `baat`=`baa`(2)+`t`(1) |
| Initial single cons | 1 | `j` in `aaj` = `aa`(2)+`j`(1) |

**Step 3 — Group into arkan (ارکان میں تقسیم)**

Look for these patterns in the weight sequence:

| Rukn | Pattern | Example Sounds |
|------|:-------:|----------------|
| فعولن fa-uu-lun | 1-2-2 | `sa-` `da-` `qat` → `sa-da-qat` |
| فاعلن faa-i-lun | 2-1-2 | `yaa-` `de-` `kaa` → `yaa-de-kaa` |
| فاعلاتن faa-i-laa-tun | 2-1-2-2 | `aa-` `ta-` `she-` `se` → `aa-ta-she-se` |
| مفاعیلن ma-faa-ii-lun | 1-2-2-2 | `bu-` `taa-` `noN-` `se` → `bu-taa-noN-se` |
| مستفعلن mus-taf-i-lun | 2-2-1-2 | — |
| مفعولات maf-uu-laat | 2-2-3 | — |

**Step 4 — Name the bahr (بحر کی پہچان)**

Match grouped arkan against Section 12.5's 14 standard bahrs:
- **Starts 2-1-2-2** → Ramal family
- **Starts 1-2-2-2** → Hazaj family  
- **Starts 1-2-2** → Mutaqarib family
- **Total weight 26** → Ramal Musamman Mahzuuf
- **Total weight 20** → Ramal/Hazaj Musaddas or Mutaqarib

**Step 5 — Verify misra-2** matches misra-1 identically.

---

#### Example 1: Bahr-e-Mutaqarib Saalim (فعولن فعولن فعولن فعولن = 1-2-2×4)

**Sher:**
```
محبت بری ہے نہ نفرت بری ہے
بری ہے تو ہر شے کی کثرت بری ہے
```

**Misra-1 syllabification:**
```
mu-hab-bat bu-rii hai na naf-rat bu-rii hai
```

| Syl | mu | hab | bat | bu | rii | hai | na | naf | rat | bu | rii | hai |
|:---:|:--:|:---:|:---:|:--:|:---:|:---:|:--:|:---:|:---:|:--:|:---:|:---:|
| Wt  | 1  | 2   | 2   | 1  | 2   | 2   | 1  | 2   | 2   | 1  | 2   | 2   |

**Group into arkan:** `1-2-2 / 1-2-2 / 1-2-2 / 1-2-2`
(mu-hab-bat) (bu-rii-hai) (na-naf-rat) (bu-rii-hai) = فعولن ×4 ✓

**Bahr:** Bahr-e-Mutaqarib Saalim ✓

---

#### Example 2: Bahr-e-Ramal Musamman Mahzuuf (فاعلاتن فاعلاتن فاعلاتن فاعلن = 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2)

**Sher:**
```
دل کی بے چینی نے اپنا کام آخر کر دیا
تیرے میرے رابطے کو عام آخر کر دیا
```

**Misra-1 syllabification & weight grouping:**
```
2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2
(dil-ki-be-chai) (nii-ne-ap-naa) (kaam-aa-khir) (kar-di-yaa)
```

> **Note on overlong syllables**: The word `kaam` (کام) is overlong (kaa=2, m=1). In scansion, the `kaa` (2) fills one syllable position of the third rukn while `m` (1) starts the sequence `m-aa-khir` = 1-2-2 within the same rukn. This is how overlong syllables naturally span rukn boundaries.

---

#### Example 3: Bahr-e-Khafiif Musaddas Makhbuun Mahzuuf Maqtu (فاعلاتن فعلاتن فعلن = 2-1-2-2 / 1-2-2 / 1-2)

The most famous ghazal by Ghalib — total weight 18 per misra.

**Sher:**
```
کوئی امید بر نہیں آتی
کوئی صورت نظر نہیں آتی
```

**Misra-1 syllabification:**
```
ko-yii-um-miid-bar-na-hiiN-aa-tii
```

| Syl | ko | yii | um | miid | bar | na | hiiN | aa | tii |
|:---:|:--:|:---:|:--:|:----:|:---:|:--:|:----:|:--:|:---:|
| Wt  | 1  | 2   | 2  | 2+1  | 2   | 1  | 2    | 2  | 2   |

**Group into arkan:** First rukn: `2-1-2-2` (Faa-i-laa-tun) with `ko-yii-um-mi` where mi(1) of miid starts the rukn... or equivalently, the overlong miid(2+1) spans the boundary.

Simpler breakdown: `2-1-2-2 / 1-2-2 / 1-2`
= (faa-i-laa-tun) + (fa-i-laa-tun) + (fa-i-lun) ✓

Transliteration: *koī ummīd bar nahīñ ātī / koī sūrat nazar nahīñ ātī*

#### Quick Taqti Troubleshooting Guide

| Problem | Likely Cause | Fix |
|---------|------------|-----|
| Too many syllables | Overlong vowel not subdivided | Split CVːC as 2+1 |
| Too few syllables | Elision or hastening | Check if a long vowel is shortened (takhfeef) |
| Pattern doesn't match any bahr | Be-behri | Rewrite the line |
| First foot matches but last doesn't | Wrong zihaaf | Try Mahzuuf or Makhbuun variant |
| Both misras different | Metrical inconsistency | One misra must be rewritten |

## 13. Forms of Urdu Poetry

### 13.1 Ghazal (غزل)
The most iconic form of Urdu poetry — a collection of 5-15 independent, thematically disconnected couplets (shers).

**Essential Rules:**
- All shers share the **same Bahr** (meter), **same Radeef** (refrain), **same Qafiya** (rhyme)
- First sher is the **Matla** (radeef + qafiya in BOTH misras)
- Last sher is the **Maqta** (contains poet's takhallus)
- No narrative thread — each sher is a self-contained poetic universe
- A ghazal has no title; it is indexed by its radeef

**Example — Ghalib's complete ghazal with radeef `nahīñ ātī`:**
```
کوئی امید بر نہیں آتی
کوئی صورت نظر نہیں آتی
...
کعبہ کس منہ سے جاؤگے غالب
شرم تم کو مگر نہیں آتی
```
Transliteration: *koī ummīd bar nahīñ ātī / ... / ka'bā kis muñh se jāoge ġhālib / sharm tum ko magar nahīñ ātī*
Bahr: Khafiif Musaddas Makhbuun Mahzuuf Maqtu (Sec 12.5 #14)
Qafiya: `ummiid`, `suurat`, `baat`, `...sharm` | Radeef: `nahiiN aatii`

### 13.2 Nazm (نظم)
A thematically unified poem with a central idea — the opposite of ghazal's disjointed structure.

**Features:**
- Has a **title** (ghazals do not)
- No mandatory radeef/qafiya (can be free verse or rhymed)
- Any meter or no meter — free verse nazm is a recognized modern form
- Can be narrative, descriptive, satirical, political, or philosophical
- One continuous argument/idea from start to finish

**Pioneers:** Akhtarul Iman, Noon Meem Rashid (modernist), Meeraji
**Masters:** Faiz Ahmed Faiz, Munir Niazi, Parveen Shakir, Iftikhar Arif

**Example — Faiz Ahmed Faiz (from *Mujh Se Pehli Si Mohabbat*):**
```
مجھ سے پہلی سی محبت مری محبوب نہ مانگ
میں نے سمجھا تھا کہ تو ہے تو درخشاں ہے حیات
تیرا غم ہے تو غمِ دہر کا جھگڑا کیا ہے
تیری صورت سے ہے عالم میں بہاروں کو ثبات
...
اور بھی دکھ ہیں زمانے میں محبت کے سوا
راحتیں اور بھی ہیں وصل کی راحت کے سوا
```
Transliteration: *mujh se pehlī sī mohabbat mirī mahbūb na māñg / ... / aur bhī dukh haiñ zamāne meñ mohabbat ke sivā / rāhaten aur bhī haiñ vasl kī rāhat ke sivā*

### 13.3 Qita (قطعہ)
A short poem of 2-6+ couplets that are **thematically connected** — unlike ghazal shers which are independent.

**Features:**
- Like a "homogeneous ghazal excerpt" — all shers on one topic
- Often moralistic, philosophical, or satirical
- May or may not follow ghazal radeef/qafiya conventions
- No matla requirement (first line doesn't need radeef in both misras)

### 13.4 Rubai (رباعی) — Quatrain
A four-line poem (quatrain) dealing with a single philosophical theme. The most compact of all Urdu forms.

**Rhyme Scheme:** A-A-B-A (mandatory) — line 3 alone breaks the rhyme. A-A-A-A is also found rarely (called *Rubai-e-Musaffa*).

**Unique Bahrs for Rubai:** Rubai has its own specific set of bahrs (24 specific patterns) that differ from ordinary ghazal bahrs. The most common are:
1. **Bahr-e-Rubai Ahmar** (احمر) — `مفاعلن مفاعلن فعولن` (1-2-1-2 / 1-2-1-2 / 1-2-2)
2. **Bahr-e-Rubai Azraq** (ازرق) — `مفعول مفاعیلن فعولن` (2-2-1 / 1-2-2-2 / 1-2-2)
3. **Bahr-e-Rubai Abyaż** (ابیض) — `مفاعلن مفاعلن فعلن` (1-2-1-2 / 1-2-1-2 / 1-2)
4. **Bahr-e-Rubai Akhżar** (اخضر) — `مفعولُ مفاعلن فعلن` (2-2-1 / 1-2-1-2 / 1-2)

**Example — Akbar Allahabadi (Urdu's Rubai master):**
```
ہر ایک سے سنا نیا فسانہ ہم نے
دیکھا دنیا میں ایک زمانہ ہم نے
اول یہ تھا کہ وقفیت پہ تھا ناز
آخر یہ کھلا کہ کچھ نہ جانا ہم نے
```
Transliteration: *har ek se sunā nayā fasāna ham ne / dekhā duniyā meñ ek zamāna ham ne / awwal ye thā ki waqfiyat pe thā nāz / ākhir ye khulā ki kuchh na jāna ham ne*
Rhyme: `-aana-ham-ne` (A, A, break, A)

**Another — Omar Khayyam's famous Persian rubai (transcreated in Urdu):**
```
اے چرخ فلک خرابی از تو ہزار
بہتر ز تو ہر کسی کہ جستم نہ بکار
آہنگ بقا ندارم زندگانی
تو نیک و بدی نمیکشی از میں ہزار
```

### 13.5 Masnavi (مثنوی)
A long narrative poem where each couplet rhymes internally (AA, BB, CC, etc.). The form for epic stories.

**Key Rules:**
- Every couplet has its OWN independent rhyme — no shared radeef/qafiya
- The rhyme changes with each new couplet: AA, BB, CC, DD...
- Any bahr, but Mutaqarib (narrative flow) and Hazaj are common
- Length: from 30 to 30,000+ couplets

**Famous Urdu Masnavis:**
| Masnavi | Author | Subject |
|---------|--------|---------|
| **Sehr-ul-Bayan** (سحر البیان) | Meer Hasan (d. 1786) | Romantic epic of a prince |
| **Gulzar-e-Naseem** (گلزار نسیم) | Daya Shankar Naseem | Romantic tale |
| **Gul-e-Bakavali** | — | Persianate romance |
| **Hatim Tai** | — | Tale of Hatim's generosity |
| **Masnavi of Meer and Sauda** | Mir Taqi Mir, Mirza Sauda | Various narrative poems |

### 13.6 Qasida (قصیدہ)
A panegyric ode, usually praising a king, noble, religious figure, or deity. The oldest Arabic poetic form adopted into Urdu.

**Structure:**
1. **Nasib/Naseeb** (نصیب) — opening section, often a love or nature theme
2. **Rahil** (رحیل) — the journey (departure from the opening theme)  
3. **Maddh** (مدح) — the praise itself (main body)
4. **Shukr/Hija** (شکر/ہجا) — thanks to God or satire of enemies
5. **Du'a** (دعا) — closing prayer for the patron

**Technical Features:**
- Same bahr throughout, often a LONG bahr (Musamman)
- Same radeef and qafiya throughout (like ghazal), but 50-100+ lines
- A qasida can run 200 lines without changing rhyme

**Masters:** Mirza Sauda (master of both praise and satire), Zauq, Ghalib, Iqbal
**Sauda's Qasida in praise of a noble:** His famous qasida runs 121 couplets all on a single rhyme.

### 13.7 Marsiya (مرثیہ)
Elegy — a mourning poem, in Urdu almost exclusively for the martyrs of Karbala (Imam Husayn and companions).

**Form:** Typically **Musaddas** (6-line stanzas): AAAABB

**Structure of a classical Marsiya:**
1. **Chehra** (چہرہ) — introduction / description of the subject
2. **Sarapa** (سراپا) — physical description of the hero
3. **Rukhsat** (رخصت) — the battle scenes and death
4. **Bain** (بین) — lamentation and mourning
5. **Du'a** (دعا) — prayer

**The Four Pillars of Marsiya:**
| Aspect | Mir Anees | Mirza Dabir |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Era** | 1803-1874 | 1803-1875 |
| **Style** | Emotional, lush imagery, heart-wrenching | Grand, martial, epic scale |
| **Vocabulary** | Accessible Urdu | Persianized, complex |
| **Focus** | Human suffering, pathos | Heroic grandeur, divine justice |

**Example — Mir Anees:**
```
دشتِ وغا میں نورِ خدا کا ظہور ہے
ذروں میں روشنیِ تجلّیِ طور ہے
...
اللہ رے حسنِ طبقہ عنبر سرشت کا
میدانِ کربلا ہے نمونہ بہشت کا
```
Transliteration: *dasht-e-vaghā meñ nūr-e-ḳhudā kā zuhūr hai / zarroñ meñ raushnī-e-tajallī-e-Ṭūr hai / ... allāh re husn-e-tabqa-e-ambar-sarishṭ kā / maidān-e-Karbalā hai namūna-e-bihisht kā*

### 13.8 Musaddas (مسدس)
A stanzaic form: each stanza has 6 lines (mesra), rhyming AAAABB.

**Lines 1-4:** Build a theme (rhyme A)
**Lines 5-6:** Conclusive couplet (rhyme B)
**Stanzas:** All share the SAME B-rhyme throughout the poem

**Most Famous Example:**
Musaddas-e-Hali (*Madd-o-Jazr-e-Islam*) by Altaf Hussain Hali — "The Flow and Ebb of Islam"
- 293 six-line stanzas
- Traces Islamic history from rise to decline
- Pioneered the use of Musaddas for social reform

### 13.9 Mukhammas (مخمس)
A five-line stanza form (from Arabic *khums* = five).

**Stanza Structure:**
- First stanza: All **5 lines rhyme** (AAAAA)
- Subsequent stanzas: First **4 lines rhyme together** (BBBB A, CCCC A, etc.)
- The **5th line** of every stanza carries the SAME rhyme as the first stanza's lines

**Feature:** The repeating 5th line (called **Tukbandi** / تکبندی) creates continuity.
**Use:** Devotional poetry, Sufi qawwalis.
**Masters:** Momin Khan Momin, Khwaja Meer Dard.

### 13.10 Hamd (حمد)
Poem in praise of Allah (God). Typically opens any formal mushaira.

**Features:**
- Any form (ghazal, nazm, rubai, qasida)
- Always begins with `Al-hamdu lillah...` or similar praise formula
- Focus: Divine attributes, creation, mercy, power

**Masters:** Iqbal, Shibli Nomani, Hasrat Mohani, Shabbir Hasan Khan

### 13.11 Naat (نعت)
Poem in praise of Prophet Muhammad (PBUH).

**Features:**
- Deeply devotional, often recited at Milad gatherings
- Strictly follows classical norms: bahr, radeef, qafiya
- Avoids shirk (associating partners with God) — praise is within Islamic bounds

**Masters:** Ahmad Raza Khan Barelvi, Maulana Ameer Ullah Sohail, Allama Iqbal

### 13.12 Manqabat (منقبت)
Sufi devotional poem praising Ali ibn Abi Talib or other Sufi saints (awliya).

**Features:**
- Formally identical to ghazal/nazm
- Content focuses on spiritual lineage, miracles, virtues of the saint
- Common in Shia and Sufi traditions

**Masters:** Maulana Sadiq, various Sufi poets of the Deccan and Awadh.

### 13.13 Rekhti (ریختی)
A unique genre where male poets wrote in a **female voice** (women's language, dialect, and perspective).

**Historical Significance:**
- Pioneered by **Rangin** (Sayyad Insha Allah Khan, d. 1837) and **Insha**
- 18th-19th century Delhi/Lucknow
- Recorded women's dialect, domestic life, social concerns
- Used female persona for both love poetry and social commentary
- Sometimes erotic, often bold and unapologetic

**Features:**
- Language: women's colloquial Urdu (not Persianized)
- Themes: domestic life, female friendships, love, fashion, gossip
- Form: ghazal structure with same metrical requirements
- Name literally means "mixed/bastard" — considered transgressive

**Example (Rangin):**
```
ہم کو تجھ سے کیا ہو بھلا تو بھی نہیں
اور نہ ہیں یار تمہارے کوئی اور بھی نہیں
```

**Note:** After the 1857 rebellion, Rekhti fell out of favor as Urdu poetry became more "respectable."

### 13.14 Doha (دوہا)
Two-line folk couplet from the Hindi/Sant tradition, adopted into Urdu folk poetry.

**Structure:**
- Each line has 24 matras (morae) = total 48 matras per doha
- Rhyme scheme: AA
- Meter: any meter, but typically the same line-internal pattern
- Language: simple, often uses Braj/Hindi vocabulary

**Example (Kabir-style moral doha in Urdu):**
```
دھیرے دھیرے آو رے پنچھی دھیرے دھیرے آ
اپنی سی تو اڑ جائے گا مجھ کو تڑپ نہ جا
```
Transliteration: *dhīre dhīre āv re pañchhī dhīre dhīre ā / apnī sī to uṛ jāegā mujh ko taṛap na jā*

### 13.15 Geet (گیت)
Song-like poem meant to be sung, often about love, nature, or seasons.

**Features:**
- Irregular meter allowed (melody takes precedence over scansion)
- Refrain (Tuk / ٹیک) repeated at intervals
- Often describes monsoon (sawan), spring (basant), or festivals
- Common in Hindi-Urdu film music and folk traditions

### 13.16 Kafi (کافی)
A form popular in Punjabi/Siraiki Sufi poetry.

**Features:**
- Short stanzas with a refrain
- Set to music (sung in qawwali style)
- Mystical, exploring themes of divine love, separation from the Beloved (God)
- Masters: **Bulleh Shah** (1680-1757), **Shah Hussain** (1538-1599), **Sultan Bahoo** (1630-1691)

### 13.17 Munajat (مناجات)
An intimate, deeply personal prayer-poem addressed to God.

**Features:**
- Highly emotional, pleading, vulnerable tone
- Any form (usually short nazm or ghazal)
- NOT the same as Hamd (which is praise) — Munajat is a whispered request
- Often written in times of personal crisis or spiritual seeking

### Collection Forms

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Diwan** (دیوان) | Complete collection of ghazals by one poet (alphabetically by radeef) | *Diwan-e-Ghalib* (contains ~235 ghazals, ~1000 couplets) |
| **Kulliyat** (کلیات) | Complete works of a poet (ghazals + nazms + masnavis + all forms) | *Kulliyat-e-Iqbal* |
| **Intikhab** (انتخاب) | Selected works (anthology) | *Intikhab-e-Meer* |
| **Salam** (سلام) | Shia devotional poem greeting/saluting the Prophet or Imams | |
| **Noha** (نوحہ) | Elegiac poem mourning the martyrs of Karbala, often in Marsiya meters | |
| **Shehr Ashob** (شہر آشوب) | Poem lamenting the decline of a city/society; sociopolitical critique | |
| **Sehra** (سہرا) | Wedding poem, sung during the marriage ceremony | |
| **Hazal** (ہزل) | Comic/humorous poetry, often with double entendre | |
| **Hijv** (ہجو) | Satire/invective — mocking a person or institution | Sauda's famous hijv |
| **Wasokht** (وسوخت) | "Disenchantment" poem — renouncing the beloved, a mini-genre | |
| **Triveni** (تریونی) | Three-line stanza (a modern form) | |

## 14. Poetic Devices (Sanat / صنائع بدیع و بیان)

Urdu poetry employs two branches of rhetorical devices:
- **Ilm-e-Bayaan** (علم بیان) — devices of meaning (tashbeeh, istea'ra, kinaaya)
- **Ilm-e-Badee'** (علم بدیع) — devices of form/embellishment (talmeeh, iham, mubaligha, tajnis, etc.)

### 14.1 Tashbeeh (تشبیہ) — Simile
A direct comparison using explicit words like *jaise* (جیسے), *maaNind* (مانند), *sa* (سا), *misaal* (مثال).

**Structure:** مشبّہ (mushabbah — the object being compared) + ادات تشبیہ (adatus tashbeeh — comparison word) + مشبّہ بہ (mushabbah beh — what it's being compared to).

**Example — Ghalib:**
```
نہ تھا کچھ تو خدا تھا کچھ نہ ہوتا تو خدا ہوتا
ڈوبویا مجھ کو ہونے نے نہ ہوتا میں تو کیا ہوتا
```
`ne thā kuchh to ḳhudā thā kuchh na hotā to ḳhudā hotā` — the entire sher works as an extended tashbeeh of existence vs non-existence.

**Example — Meer:**
```
چاند سے پوچھو کہ اے ماہ جمال
کیوں گراں ہے ترا گھونگھٹ وال
```
`chānd se pūchho ki ai māh-e-jamāl` — addressing the beloved as literally the moon (tashbeeh through direct address).

**Example of a classic simple tashbeeh:**
اس کا چہرہ چاند جیسا ہے
*us kā chehrā chānd jaisā hai* (Her face is like the moon)
— Here `chehrā` (face) = مشبّہ, `jaisā` = ادات تشبیہ, `chānd` (moon) = مشبّہ بہ

### 14.2 Istea'ra (استعارہ) — Metaphor
Implied comparison WITHOUT using explicit comparison words. The object IS the other thing.

**Example (Face = Moon, without saying "like"):**
```
چاند سرہانے ہے اور زلف ہے زنجیر گلو
```
`chand sarhāne hai aur zulf hai zanjīr-e-gulū` — The moon is at the bedside (the beloved's face) and the hair is a chain around the neck.

**Example — Ghalib (Heart = Prey, Love = Hunter):**
```
دل ناداں تجھے ہوا کیا ہے
عشق نے آکر تجھے زخم کیا تائی
```
*dil-e-nādān tujhe huā kyā hai / ishq ne aa kar tujhe zakhm kiyā tā* — The inexperienced heart has been wounded by love.

**Common Istea'ra in Urdu poetry:**
- `دل` (dil) = heart = a wounded bird / a mirror / a glasshouse
- `دنیا` (duniyā) = world = a playground / a caravanserai / a chessboard
- `غم` (ġham) = sorrow = night / a heavy load / a storm
- `زمانہ` (zamānah) = time = a snake / a thief / a betrayer

**Istea'ra Sub-types:**
| Type | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Tashkhees** (تشخیص) | Personification — giving human traits to objects | `شام کا سایہ` (the shadow of evening) as a living thing |
| **Tamsil** (تمثیل) | Allegory — an extended narrative metaphor | The story of the moth and the flame |
| **Tamthil** (تمثیل) | Parabolic metaphor | Iqbal's *Shikwa* structure |

### 14.3 Talmeeh (تلمیح) — Allusion
Reference to a well-known historical, religious, or mythical person/event to evoke associated meanings without elaboration.

**Structure:** One or two words that trigger an entire story or value-system in the listener's mind.

**Common allusions table:**

| Allusion | Figure/Event | Poetic Meaning |
|----------|-------------|----------------|
| **یوسف** (Yusuf) | Prophet Joseph, famed for beauty | Ultimate beauty standard |
| **مصر** (Misr) | Egypt | The place of Yusuf's trial / Zulaikha's love |
| **لیلیٰ / مجنون** (Laila/Majnun) | Classic Arab lovers | Madness of love |
| **شیریں / فرہاد** (Shirin/Farhad) | Persian lovers, Farhad's mountain-carving | Sacrifice for love |
| **کوہِ طور** (Koh-e-Toor) | Mount Sinai, where Moses saw God | Revelation, divine light |
| **کربلا** (Karbala) | Imam Husayn's martyrdom | Ultimate sacrifice, injustice |
| **نمرود** (Namrud) | Biblical king who challenged Abraham | Tyranny, arrogance |
| **آتشِ نمرود** (Aatish-e-Namrud) | Fire that became cool for Abraham | Miraculous protection |
| **فطرت** (Fitrat) / **رنگ و بو** | Creation | Earthly vs Divine |

**Example — Ghalib using Kohe Toor:**
```
ہم سے پوچھو کہ یہ الفت کا علاج اے غالب
ہم نے اک بار کہا تھا، یہ تو کوہِ طور تھا
```
*ham se pūchho ki ye ulfat kā ilāj ai Ġhālib / ham ne ek bār kahā thā, yah to koh-e-Ṭūr thā* — The mountain of Sinai (revelation/divine love) is the cure for love's affliction.

**Another — Shibli Nomani using Yusuf:**
```
اندلس میں بھی اسی شان سے گزرا ہے جوں
شہرِ مصر میں انعام یوسف کا تھا طور
```
Referencing Prophet Yusuf's reception in Egypt.

### 14.4 Kinaaya (کنایہ) — Innuendo / Metonymy
Saying one thing but meaning another — indirect expression that has become conventionally understood.

**Example:**
```
آپ کی انگلی پکڑنا
```
*āp kī ungli pakṛnā* — Literally "to hold your finger," but conventionally meaning "to follow someone's guidance."

**More examples:**
| Phrase | Literal | Figurative Meaning |
|--------|---------|-------------------|
| `لشکر سے محبت` | "mud-loving" | patriotism ("mud of one's homeland") |
| `دو گز کفن` | "two yards of shroud" | death, mortal remains |
| `پانی پھر گیا` | "the water turned" | irreversible decision |
| `ہاتھ باندھے` | "hands tied" | humility, respect |
| `دامن کشاں` | "skirting the edge" | ignoring, abandoning |

**Example — Ghalib (using kinaaya for death):**
```
دل ڈوبتا ہے کسی کے دکھ کا جب آتا ہے
سمجھو کسی کی آنکھ کا پانی اتر گیا
```
*dil ḍobtā hai kisī ke dukh kā jab ātā hai / samjho kisī kī ānkh kā pānī utar gayā* — "When someone's grief comes, the heart sinks / Know that the water has run out from someone's eyes" → kinaaya for death.

### 14.5 Iham (ایہام) — Ambiguity / Double Entendre
A deliberate pun or ambiguity where one word carries TWO meanings simultaneously, and BOTH are poetically valid. Ghalib's most characteristic device.

**Example — Ghalib's famous use of `sharar`:**
```
نقش فریادی ہے کس کی شوخی تحریر کا
کاغذی ہے پیرہن ہر پیکر تصویر کا
```
Here `kāġhżī` (paper-like) simultaneously refers to the flimsy paper garment of the portrait AND the fragile nature of worldly existence.

**Another — Ghalib (the word `ishrat`):**
```
عشرتِ قطرہ ہے دریا میں فنا ہو جانا
درد کا حد سے گزرنا ہے دوا ہو جانا
```
*ishrat-e-qaṭrah hai dariyā meñ fanā ho jānā / dard kā had se guzarnā hai dawā ho jānā* — The drop's pleasure is to die in the ocean / The surpassing of pain is to become the cure. `Ishrat` = pleasure/joy, but also hints at spending/loss.

**Common Iham-words:**
| Word | Meaning 1 | Meaning 2 |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| `شور` (shor) | Noise/commotion | Saltiness (of tears) |
| `ناز` (nāz) | Coyness/coquetry | Pride |
| `خمار` (khumār) | Hangover | After-effect of love |
| `نشہ` (nashā) | Intoxication | Spiritual ecstasy |
| `غم` (ġham) | Sorrow | Care/concern |

### 14.6 Mubaligha (مبالغہ) — Hyperbole
Deliberate exaggeration for rhetorical effect.

**Example — Meer:**
```
وہ نفرتیں تھیں کہ پھر مطلق نمی ملے
یا یوں ہی رہا ہوں میں کہ پھر کبھی نہ ملے
```
*vo nafrateñ thīñ ki phir muṭlaq na mīle / yā yūñ hī rahā hūñ meñ ki phir kabhī na mile* — Exaggeration of total separation.

**Example — Hasrat Mohani:**
```
چپکے چپکے رات دن آنسو بہانا یاد ہے
ہم کو اب تک عاشقی کا وہ زمانہ یاد ہے
```
*chupke chupke rāt din ānsū bahānā yād hai / ham ko ab tak āshiqī kā vuh zamānah yād hai* — Hyperbolic: crying secretly day AND night; entire era remembered.

**Common hyperbole types in Urdu poetry:**
- The beloved's eyelash is an arrow
- The poet will endure 1000 pains
- Mountains melt from the poet's sighs
- Time itself stops at the beloved's glance

### 14.7 Husn-e-Matla (حسن مطلع) — Beauty of the Opening

A **Husn-e-Matla** is a matla so striking, beautiful, or original that it becomes the defining feature of the ghazal. A ghazal can have:
- **Matla-e-Awwal** (مطلع اول) — The first matla
- **Husn-e-Matla** (حسن مطلع) — A second matla in the same ghazal, equally beautiful
- **Matla-e-Saani** (مطلع ثانی) — Another opening-equivalent sher later in the ghazal
- **Zaib-e-Matla** (ذیب مطلع) — An ornamented matla (a specific type of Husn-e-Matla)

**Example of a Husn-e-Matla (Ghalib):**
```
بازیچہ اطفال ہے دنیا مرے آگے
ہوتا ہے شب و روز تماشا مرے آگے
```
*bāzīcha-e-aṭfāl hai duniyā mire āge / hotā hai shab-o-roz tamāshā mire āge* — The world is a child's plaything before me / Night and day it performs a spectacle before me. The matla immediately establishes the poet's existential authority and sets up the ghazal's themes. Both misras carry the radeef (`mire āge`), making it technically a matla, but the power of the statement elevates it to **Husn-e-Matla**.

### 14.8 Mazmoon Aafrini / Mazmoon Bandi (مضمون آفرینی) — Thematic Innovation

The art of expressing a **well-worn theme in a completely fresh way**. This is the highest mark of poetic skill. A poet who can say "love is painful" — something said a million times — in a way no one has ever said it before, demonstrates **Mazmoon Aafrini**.

**Example of thematic innovation — Ghalib reimagining death:**
```
مرنے کی بات کہتے ہو تو یوں تو کہتے ہو
بسنے کو روز چڑھتا ہے پانی تیرا
```
*marne kī bāt kahte ho to yūñ to kahte ho / basne ko roz chaṛhtā hai pānī terā* — "When you speak of dying, say it so that your waters rise every day to settle" — Ghalib redefines death not as an end but as a rising flood, completely inverting the conventional image.

**Another — Faraz gives a new twist to separation:**
```
اب کے ہم بچھڑے تو شاید کبھی خوابوں میں ملیں
جس طرح سوکھے ہوئے پھول کتابوں میں ملیں
```
*ab ke ham bichhaṛe to shāyad kabhī ḳhvāboñ meñ mileñ / jis tarah sūkhe hue phūl kitāboñ meñ mileñ* — "Now that we've parted, perhaps we'll meet in dreams / Like dried flowers found in books" — the comparison of meeting in dreams to finding pressed flowers in an old book is a stunningly original image (the quintessence of mazmoon aafrini).

### 14.9 Radd-e-Matla (رد مطلع) — Echoing the Opening

When a sher later in the ghazal echoes the matla's imagery, sound, or meaning — creating structural symmetry. A sign of advanced craftsmanship.

### 14.10 Additional Devices

| Device | Name | Meaning | Example |
|--------|------|---------|---------|
| **تضاد** (Tazadd) | Antithesis | Juxtaposition of opposites | `خوشی و غم` (joy and sorrow) |
| **مراعات النظیر** (Mura'at-un-Nazir) | Congruence | Multiple related words in one sher | `دل` (heart), `محبت` (love), `درد` (pain) in one line |
| **حسن تعلیل** (Husn-e-Ta'leel) | Beautiful causation | Giving a fanciful reason for a phenomenon | "Stars come out because the beloved looks up" |
| **تکرار** (Takrar) | Repetition | Emphatic repetition of a word | `آگے آگے دیکھیے` (wait and see) — Meer |
| **تجنيس** (Tajnis) | Paronomasia | Two words with same spelling, different meaning | `بھلا` (good) vs `بھلا` (forget) |
| **تشخيص** (Tashkhis) | Personification | Giving human qualities to objects | `دل نے کہا` (the heart said) |
| **رفع** (Raf') | Climax | Gradual intensification | Love → madness → death |
| **التفات** (Iltifaat) | Apostrophe | Sudden shift in address | Switching from third to second person mid-sher |
| **اقتباس** (Iqtibas) | Quotation | Quoting Quran/hadith in poetry | Iqbal's frequent Quranic references |
| **تلمیح الی المجاز** (Talmeeh Il-al-Majaz) | Mythological allusion | Reference to Persian/Hindu mythology | `رستم`, `سہراب` (Rustam, Sohrab) |

## 15. Major Poets — Deep Profiles

### 15.1 Meer Taqi Meer (میر تقی میر) — 1723–1810
**Status:** Khuda-e-Sukhan (خدائے سخن — God of Poetry)
**Era:** Delhi School, 18th century Mughal India

**Style:**
- **Emotion over intellect** — raw grief, heartbreak, longing
- **Simple, natural diction** — profound meaning in everyday words
- **Dard (pain)** as central subject — heartbreak, loss of Delhi, social decay
- **Short meters** — especially Bahr-e-Ramal Musaddas Mahzuuf
- Complete self-sufficiency of each sher
- Pioneer of the Urdu ghazal as known today

**Famous ashaar:**
```
ibtidaa-e-ishq hai rotaa hai kyaa
aage aage dekhiye hotaa hai kyaa
```

### 15.2 Mirza Ghalib (مرزا غالب) — 1797–1869
**Status:** The greatest Urdu poet
**Era:** Late Mughal / Early British, Delhi

**Style:**
- **Intellectual & philosophical** — existential angst, metaphysics, paradox
- **Complex layered imagery** — each sher at multiple interpretive levels
- **Iham (wordplay & ambiguity)** — deliberate double meanings
- **Wide metrical range** — master of complex, rare bahrs
- **Persianized vocabulary** — deeper, less accessible than Meer
- Often described as harder to understand, but richer upon reflection

**Famous ashaar:**
```
hazāroN ḳhvāhish aisi ki har ḳhvāhish pe dam nikle
bahut nikle mire armān lekin phir bhī kam nikle
```
```
na thā kuchh to ḳhudā thā, kuchh na hotā to ḳhudā hotā
Duboyā mujh ko hone ne, na hotā maiñ to kyā hotā
```

**Ghalib's Diwan:** Contains ~235 ghazals and ~1000 couplets total. His Persian Diwan is 5× larger.

### 15.5 Shibli Nomani (شبلی نعمانی) — 1857–1914
**Status:** Founder of modern Urdu literary criticism; historian, Islamic scholar, poet
**Era:** British India (Aligarh Movement)

**Contribution:**

## 16. How to Write a Proper Sher — Step-by-Step

1. **Choose your Bahr** — Start with Bahr-e-Ramal Musaddas Mahzuuf (easiest): `2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2`
2. **Fix your Radeef** — Pick a phrase that can end any line naturally (e.g., `nahiiN hai`, `karo`, `huaa`)
3. **Fix your Qafiya sound** — Decide the rhyme (e.g., `-aar`, `-ish`, `-ii`)
4. **Write the Matla** — Both lines rhyme. First line establishes the musical pattern.
5. **Write subsequent shers** — Second line must rhyme with the Matla. First lines are free (same bahr).
6. **Taqti every line** — Break into syllables and verify weight matches chosen bahr.
7. **Ensure independence** — Each sher must stand alone. Don't continue a thought across shers.
8. **End with Maqta** — Insert your takhallus in the last sher.

### Worked Example: Correcting a Sher

**Original (be-behr — broken meter):**
```
Hai ye hasrat k mulaqat ho jaye
Tum ao ham bethe Kuch pal baat ho jaye
```
**Problems:** Line 1=11 positions, Line 2=12 (different meter). Grammar: `k`→`ki`.

**Corrected (Bahr-e-Ramal Musaddas Mahzuuf — 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2-2 / 2-1-2):**

> **ہے یہ حسرت اور کوئی رات ہو جائے**  
> **تم کو آؤ تو کوئی بات ہو جائے**

**Taqti:**
```
ہے یہ حسرت اور کوئی رات ہو جائے
 2  1 2-2  2   1-2  2   2  1-2   ← 11 positions ✓

تم کو آؤ تو کوئی بات ہو جائے
 2  1 2-1 2   1-2  2   2  1-2   ← 11 positions ✓
```
**Radeef:** ہو جائے | **Qafiya:** رات ~ بات (sound: -aat) | **Waqf:** Final ye = 2 via pause convention.

### Common Pitfalls — Detailed Error Analysis

| # | Error | Urdu Name | Why It's Wrong | Example of Error |
|:-:|-------|-----------|---------------|------------------|
| 1 | **No meter / wrong meter** | **بے بحری** (Be-Behrī) | The line's syllable weight doesn't match any bahr — cannot be sung/recited musically. Most common beginner mistake. | Writing: *تمہارے بغیر زندگی ایک کھلا ہوا زخم ہے* — try scanning: `tu-mhā-re-ba-ġhair-zin-da-gī-ek-khu-lā-hu-ā-za-khm-hai` — weight pattern 2-1-2-1-2-2-1-2-2-1-2-1-2-2-1-2 doesn't match any standard bahr. |
| 2 | **Qafiya mismatch** | **قافیہ کا فرق** (Qāfiye kā Farq) | The rhyme sound changes between shers. All qafiya words must share the same final rhyme (last consonant + preceding vowel). | Qafiya set: `پیار` (pyār), `بار` (bār), `گلزار` (gulzār) = ✓ all end in `-ār` / Qafiya set: `پیار` (pyār), `درد` (dard), `یاد` (yād) = ✗ different rhymes |
| 3 | **Radeef mismatch** | **ردیف کا فرق** (Radeef kā Farq) | The refrain phrase changes between shers. Every sher's second misra must end with the EXACT same radeef — same word, same form, same gender/number. | ✓ Radeef `نہیں آتی`: seen in every second misra of Ghalib's ghazal / ✗ Radeef changing: first sher ends `نہیں آتا`, second sher ends `نہیں آتی` |
| 4 | **Cross-sher dependency** | **انحصار** (Inhisār) | A sher needs the previous one to make sense. Each sher must be a completely self-contained unit of meaning. | Writing a thought that runs across 3 shers as if they were a nazm. You should be able to shuffle shers randomly and each still makes sense. |
| 5 | **Filler words / padding** | **حشو** (Hashw) | Words added ONLY to fix meter, carrying no poetic meaning. "Says nothing beautifully." | Adding `تھا` (thā), `ہے` (hai), `کیا` (kyā) unnecessarily just to complete a rukn. |
| 6 | **Unnatural word breaks** | **قطع کلام** (Qat'-e-Kalām) | Breaking a word or compound unnaturally at the cesura (boundary between arkan). | Crushing a word like `محبت` (mu-hab-bat) with a rukn break between `mu` and `habbat` when the meter demands it. |
| 7 | **Forced scansion** | **زحمت تقطیع** (Zehmat-e-Taqti) | Words visibly stretched or shortened unnaturally — the line "feels" wrong when read aloud even if it technically scans. | Holding a vowel too long or compressing multiple syllables into one beat. |
| 8 | **Identical qafiya** | **قافیہ کا تکرار** (Qāfiye kā Takrār) | Using the SAME qafiya word in two different shers. Each qafiya word in a ghazal must be unique. | `غبار` used in sher 2, then `غبار` used again in sher 5. |
| 9 | **Repeated rhyme word** | — | Using the same word for both qafiya AND radeef. The qafiya must be distinct from the radeef. | Radeef is `آتی` and qafiya is also `آتی` — impossible by definition. |
| 10 | **Be-wazni (wrong weight)** | **بے وزنی** (Be-Waznī) | The qafiya words don't share the same metrical weight (wazn) even though they rhyme. In Urdu, rhyme alone is insufficient — the qafiya must also have the identical syllable count and weight pattern. | `راز` (rāz, weight 2+1=3) and `آواز` (āvāz, weight 2-1+1=4) rhyme but have different weights → invalid as qafiya |
| 11 | **Matla without radeef** | — | First sher of ghazal not having radeef in BOTH misras. A proper matla MUST have radeef in both lines. | Writing matla as: *سن کے درد کی کہانی ہنس دیا* (line 1: no radeef) / *دل کی بے چینی نہ پوچھو یا رب* (line 2: different ending) |
| 12 | **Wrong bahr across shers** | **بحر کا فرق** (Bahr kā Farq) | Each sher in a ghazal must be in the EXACT same bahr. A single metrically inconsistent sher ruins the entire ghazal. | Sher 1 in Ramal Musamman (weight 26), Sher 2 accidentally shifts to Hazaj Musaddas (weight 20). |
| 13 | **Cutting grammar** | — | Breaking words unnaturally across the cesura, creating grammatical nonsense. | Splitting a compound verb unnecessarily: `کر` in rukn 1 and `دیا` in rukn 2 when they form `کردیا`. |
| 14 | **Takhfeef overuse** | **تخفیف** (Takhfeef) | Overusing syllable shortening ("hastening") — legitimate poetic license but excessive use marks amateur work. | Shortening EVERY long syllable in a line because the bahr demands too many weight-1 positions. |

#### Diagnostic Checklist for Evaluating a Ghazal

When evaluating Urdu poetry, check these in order:

1. **Bahr Correctness:** Scan one misra with taqti. Does the weight pattern match a standard bahr? (Section 12.5)
2. **Bahr Consistency:** Does every sher in the ghazal use the same bahr?
3. **Radeef Integrity:** Does every second misra (and both matla misras) end with the identical radeef?
4. **Qafiya Uniformity:** Does every qafiya word rhyme AND share the same metrical weight?
5. **Qafiya Uniqueness:** Is each qafiya word different from all others?
6. **Sher Independence:** Does each sher make complete sense on its own?
7. **Matla Presence:** Does the first sher have radeef + qafiya in BOTH misras?
8. **Maqta Context:** If a maqta is used, does it contain the takhallus naturally?
9. **Natural Flow:** Does the line sound natural when recited aloud (not just scan on paper)?
10. **Mazmoon:** Does the sher say something fresh, or is it a cliché repeated verbatim?

### Advanced Pitfall: The "ho jaye" Radeef Challenge

Some radeef phrases create a metrical surprise: `ho jaye` (ہو جائے) naturally scans as `ho(2) jaa(2) ye(1)` — ending with a **short (1)** syllable. Most standard Urdu bahrs expect the **final position** of a misra to be **long (2)**.

**Why this matters:** If you choose `ho jaye` as your radeef, you restrict yourself to bahrs where the last foot accepts a final 1, OR you must invoke **Waqf (وقف)** — the pause convention that neutralizes the final syllable (treating 1 as 2 at line-end). Waqf is widely accepted in all schools of Urdu prosody but should be used intentionally, not as a crutch.

**Diagnosis:** If a sher scans correctly except for the final syllable being 1 vs 2, waqf is the solution. If there are other mismatches, the bahr is broken.

**Solutions:**
- If keeping `ho jaye`: use waqf, or choose a bahr ending in `1-2` or `2-1-1` (e.g., certain modified feet)
- If avoiding waiver: choose naturally long-ending radeef like `nahiiN hai` (2+1-2), `karo` (2-2), `huaa` (2-2)

## 17. Mushaira Culture

**Mushaira** (مشاعرہ) — A formal gathering where poets recite their work before an audience.

**Structure:**
- Often begins with **Hamd** (praise of God) and **Naat** (praise of Prophet)
- Senior poets or starters begin
- Each poet recites 3-5 ghazals / shers
- Audience responds with **"Wah!"** (bravo!), **"Mukarrar!"** (encore!), **"Kya baat hai!"**
- Tarannum (melodic recitation) is common — a sing-song intonation that follows the meter
- The most prestigious mushairas include the doyen of poets as **Sadr-e-Mushaira** (presiding poet)
- Famous historic mushairas: **Fort William College**, **Anjuman-e-Urdu**, mushairas of **Aligarh**
